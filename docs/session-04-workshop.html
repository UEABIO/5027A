Calculating t

This page summarises what we covered in the first hour of Session 4. Use it for revision; the full activities were worked through in class.

Progression: null hypothesis → simulate under H₀ → null distribution → observed statistic → p-value → estimate ± SE → coefficient table → the linear model as generalisation.

Retrieval and ranking: round 1

We began with three scenarios. In each, the difference between two group means was the same (2.0 units), but the standard error of that difference varied:

Scenario Difference SE
A 2.0 0.5
B 2.0 1.0
C 2.0 2.0

Each bench ranked the three by strength of evidence for a real difference. Most benches arrived at A > B > C: the same difference is more convincing when measured more precisely.

Check your understanding. In Scenario C, the t-statistic is .

t = 2.0 / 2.0 = 1.0. Compare this with Scenario A, where t = 4.0. The ratio, not the estimate alone, determines the evidence.

Retrieval and ranking: round 2

The second round held the standard error constant and varied the difference:

Scenario Difference SE
D 0.5 1.0
E 2.0 1.0
F 5.0 1.0

Benches ranked D < E < F. A larger difference, at the same level of precision, provides stronger evidence.

NoteThe rule

The strength of evidence depends on the ratio of the estimate to its standard error. This ratio is the t-statistic, and it is the number the coefficient table reports. Round 1 showed that precision matters; round 2 showed that the size of the difference matters. The t-statistic captures both.

Check your understanding. Two studies report the same t-statistic of 3.0. Study 1 has a difference of 6.0 and an SE of 2.0. Study 2 has a difference of 0.3 and an SE of 0.1. Do the two studies provide the same strength of evidence against the null?

The t-statistic determines the p-value, so the two studies provide the same strength of evidence against the null hypothesis that the difference is zero. Whether the biological effect is important is a separate question — that depends on the magnitude of the difference, not on the t-value. This distinction was previewed in Session 3 (item 3.14: significance is not importance).

Reading a coefficient table on paper

We then read a coefficient table from a study comparing wing length across three Drosophila species (A, B, C), with species A as the reference level:

Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>
(Intercept) 2.10 0.08 26.25 < 0.001
speciesB 0.35 0.11 3.18 0.004
speciesC −0.12 0.11 −1.09 0.284

We identified: the intercept is the mean wing length of species A; the coefficient for speciesB is the estimated difference between B and A (B is 0.35 mm longer); the coefficient for speciesC is the estimated difference between C and A (C is 0.12 mm shorter, but the evidence is weak). The table has three rows: one intercept plus one row per non-reference level.

Check your understanding. What is the estimated mean wing length of species B?

The mean of species B is the intercept plus the coefficient for speciesB: 2.10 + 0.35 = 2.45. To recover the mean of any level, add its coefficient to the intercept.

Check your understanding. The table does not include a direct comparison between species B and species C. Why not?

The model reports every level’s difference from the reference (A), not every pairwise comparison. To compare B and C, you could change the reference level (Session 6) or compute the contrast from the existing coefficients: 0.35 − (−0.12) = 0.47. The linear model framework handles this, but the default table shows only differences from the reference.

The connection to Sessions 2 and 3

In Sessions 2 and 3 you worked with the paired differences in Darwin’s maize data. You computed the mean difference, its standard error, and its confidence interval (Session 2), and tested whether the difference was large enough to reject the null (Session 3). The intercept-only linear model lm(difference ~ 1) produces the same numbers in the coefficient table. The t-statistic in that table is the same ratio — estimate divided by standard error — that you used in the ranking exercise.

The advantage of the linear model is that the same framework extends to three groups (the paper exercise above), continuous predictors, and combinations of both. You will use this extension in Sessions 6 and 7.
