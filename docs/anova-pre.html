Learning outcomes

By the end of this week you should be able to:

  1. split the variation in a data set into a part explained by the groups and a part left over within them;

  2. calculate degrees of freedom, mean squares and F by hand, interpret F as signal divided by noise, and calculate R² from the sums of squares;

  3. use residual plots and QQ plots to judge whether the assumptions of an ANOVA model are reasonable, building on last week’s residual checks.

Reading: from group means to F

One model, one question

You have already fitted linear models with a categorical predictor. In Darwin’s maize data the predictor had two levels, and the model compared the two group means. A one-way ANOVA is the same kind of model with one categorical predictor, whatever the number of groups. It asks a single question: do the group means differ by more than we would expect from the variation within the groups?

Splitting the variation

Every observation sits some distance from the grand mean, the mean of all observations. That distance can be split into two parts:

  • the distance from the grand mean to the observation’s group mean (the part explained by the group), and
  • the distance from the group mean to the observation itself (the part left over, which is the residual).

For a group mean of 25, a grand mean of 20 and an observation of 28, the total distance is 8, of which 5 is explained by the group and 3 is the residual.

Squaring these distances and adding them across all observations gives three sums of squares:

\[ SS_{total} = SS_{group} + SS_{residual} \]

\(SS_{residual}\) is the sum of squared residuals you met last week. Some books abbreviate “sum of squares for regression” to SSR as well, so this module writes \(SS_{group}\) and \(SS_{residual}\) to avoid any confusion.

Degrees of freedom, mean squares and F

A sum of squares grows with the number of observations, so each is divided by its degrees of freedom to give a mean square (MS), an estimate of variance. For \(k\) groups and \(N\) observations:

Source Degrees of freedom Mean square
Group \(k - 1\) \(SS_{group} / (k - 1)\)
Residual \(N - k\) \(SS_{residual} / (N - k)\)

The F statistic is the ratio of the two mean squares:

\[ F = \frac{MS_{group}}{MS_{residual}} \]

The numerator is the variation between the group means (the signal). The denominator is the variation within the groups (the noise). If the group means differ only by chance, F is usually close to 1. A large F is evidence against the null hypothesis that all the group means are equal.

The p-value is the area to the right of F in an F-distribution with \(k - 1\) and \(N - k\) degrees of freedom. Only the right-hand tail is used, because F is built from squared differences, so every kind of difference between the means makes F larger.

R² and F and t

The proportion of the variation explained by the groups is

\[ R^2 = \frac{SS_{group}}{SS_{total}} \]

which has the same meaning as R² in last week’s regression. With two groups, F is exactly the square of the t statistic from a t-test that pools the variance, and the two tests give the same p-value.

A significant F says that at least one group mean differs. It does not say which one.

Checking the model

Last week you used a residual plot to check whether a straight line described the data. The same residuals are used to check an ANOVA model, which assumes that:

  • the residuals are roughly normally distributed (check with a QQ plot);
  • the spread of the residuals is similar in every group (check with a plot of residuals against fitted values, which shows one vertical stripe per group);
  • the observations are independent (this depends on how the study was designed, and cannot be seen on a plot).

A check for a straight line is not needed when the only predictor is a group label, because every group has its own mean.
