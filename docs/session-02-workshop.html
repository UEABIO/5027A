Building the confidence interval

This page summarises what we covered in the first hour of Session 2. Use it for revision; the full activities were worked through in class.

Progression: SD of the data → SE of the mean → confidence interval.

Recovering the mechanism from Session 1

We began by returning to the photograph of the Session 1 board. Each dot on that board was one group’s sample mean from the bean tub, and the spread of dots was the sampling distribution of the mean.

Two numbers were displayed alongside the photograph:

  • the standard deviation of the individual bean weights (from the tub),
  • the standard deviation of the class means (from the dots on the board).

The first number was larger than the second. Averaging cancels out extreme values: a mean of twenty beans cannot be as extreme as the most extreme single bean, so the class means clustered more tightly than the raw weights.

NoteThe rule

The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the mean is the standard error of the mean (SE). It is not a separate quantity from the standard deviation; it is what you get when you compute the SD of the sample means rather than the SD of the individual values.

From a single sample, the SE is computed directly from the sample SD:

\[ \text{SE} = \frac{\text{SD}}{\sqrt{n}} \]

The Session 1 board made the SE physical. We did not compute it, because we already had it: it was the spread of the dots the class had drawn.

Check your understanding. A colleague reports the standard deviation of blood pressure readings from 100 patients. This number describes .

The SD of the readings describes how variable blood pressure is from one patient to the next. The SE (which is \(\text{SD}/\sqrt{100}\)) would describe the precision of the sample mean.

Computing the standard error for Darwin’s maize

We then applied the formula to the dataset introduced in the pre-class reading: the 15 paired height differences from Darwin’s maize experiment.

You were given the standard deviation of the 15 differences (4.72 inches). At the bench you computed the standard error as:

\[ \text{SE} = \frac{4.72}{\sqrt{15}} \approx 1.22 \text{ inches} \]

This is what the sampling distribution of the mean difference would look like if Darwin had repeated the whole experiment many times, each time with 15 new pairs. The sample means would be spread around the true mean with a standard deviation of about 1.22 inches.

This number is the same quantity you saw as the spread of dots on the Session 1 board, but computed from a single sample rather than from many.

Check your understanding. A dataset has SD = 8 and \(n\) = 64. The SE is .

\(\text{SE} = 8 / \sqrt{64} = 8 / 8 = 1\).

From standard error to confidence interval

We paused to think about what the SE is a ruler for. The point estimate is the mean of the 15 differences. The SE tells us how far this estimate is likely to sit from the true mean. The question is: what range of values for the true mean is consistent with the estimate we obtained?

The sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal, and 95% of a normal distribution lies within about two standard deviations of its centre. Since the SE is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution, we get:

  • 95% of sample means fall within about 2 × SE of the true mean.
  • Equivalently: the true mean falls within about 2 × SE of about 95% of sample means.

The second statement is what the confidence interval expresses.

Constructing the confidence interval

NoteThe formula

A 95% confidence interval for the mean is:

\[ \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \times \text{SE} \]

where \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean and SE is the standard error. The multiplier 1.96 is the exact value from the normal distribution that puts 95% of the distribution within \(\pm 1.96\) SDs of the centre; the value 2 is close enough for a quick check. For small samples we use a slightly larger multiplier from the t-distribution, introduced in Session 3.

Substituting the Darwin numbers on the board:

\[ 95\% \text{ CI} = 2.62 \pm 1.96 \times 1.22 \approx [0.2, 5.0] \text{ inches} \]

Every quantity in this interval was built from what you already had. The sample mean (2.62) is the average of the 15 differences. The SE (1.22) is \(\text{SD}/\sqrt{15}\). The multiplier (1.96) comes from the normal distribution.

Check your understanding. A study reports a mean of 12.0, an SD of 6.0, and \(n\) = 36. The SE is and the approximate 95% CI runs from to .

\(\text{SE} = 6.0/\sqrt{36} = 1.0\). The 95% CI is \(12.0 \pm 1.96 \times 1.0\), which is approximately 10.0 to 14.0.

Consolidation: the first eight pairs

To fix the mechanism, we repeated the construction on a subset of Darwin’s data: the first eight pairs only. You were given the mean and SD of these eight differences and, at the bench, computed:

  1. the SE (SD divided by \(\sqrt{8}\)),
  2. the 95% CI (mean \(\pm\) 1.96 \(\times\) SE).

The resulting interval was wider than the interval from all 15 pairs. This is not a mistake. It reflects the \(\sqrt{n}\) relationship directly: with fewer observations, the SE is larger, so the interval is wider. Hour 2 confirms this pattern by simulation.

Check your understanding. If you compute a 95% CI from a smaller subset of the same dataset (same SD, smaller \(n\)), the interval will typically be .

Decreasing \(n\) increases the SE (\(\text{SE} = \text{SD}/\sqrt{n}\)), so the interval gets wider. This is the same \(\sqrt{n}\) relationship you will confirm by simulation in hour 2.

What the confidence interval does not claim

We closed hour 1 by flagging three plausible-sounding interpretations of “95% confidence”, all of which are wrong. The correct interpretation, and why these three fail, is the topic of hour 2, where the coverage simulation makes the 95% claim concrete. Keep the answer you wrote for the pre-class commitment task to hand; you will use it in hour 2.

The three claims to be wary of:

  • 95% probability that the true mean lies in this specific interval,
  • 95% of the data lie in this interval,
  • 95% chance that a future sample mean will fall in this interval.

Each of these is a natural first guess. None is what a frequentist confidence interval says.

Exit ticket

The question posed at the end of the session (end of hour 2):

You have a 95% confidence interval of [0.2, 5.0] for the mean difference in height between cross-fertilised and self-fertilised maize. A colleague claims the true difference is zero: that cross-fertilisation has no effect. Does your interval answer whether they are wrong, and if not, what would you need?
