Regression - Pre-reading

Complete this material before your workshop session. It carries the vocabulary you will need in the room.

Video: from two groups to a line

NoteWatch before class

After watching, you should be able to explain what the slope of a regression line measures when the predictor is a measurement rather than a group label.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this week you should be able to:

  1. interpret the slope of a regression line as the change in the response for a one-unit increase in the predictor, in the units of the data;

  2. calculate a fitted value and a residual by hand;

  3. explain why least squares uses squared residuals to choose a line;

  4. interpret the intercept, and explain how centring the predictor makes it meaningful;

  5. use a residual plot to judge whether a straight line describes the data adequately;

  6. choose between a confidence interval and a prediction interval, and recognise when a prediction is an extrapolation.

Reading: fitting a straight line

From two groups to a measurement

In Session 4 you coded Darwin’s two groups as x = 0 (Cross) and x = 1 (Self) and drew a line between their means. The intercept was the mean of the reference group, and the slope, −2.62 inches, was the difference between the two means. That x-axis was a label: no plant sits halfway between cross- and self-pollinated.

In regression, the predictor is a measurement. Every value between the observed ones means something, so the line describes how the response changes across the whole range of the predictor. The slope is now the change in the response for a one-unit increase in the predictor.

The model is fitted with the same function, and its output has the same coefficient table:

lm(hardness ~ dens, data = janka)

The response goes on the left of the ~ and the predictor on the right. The row labelled (Intercept) is the intercept, and the row labelled dens is the slope.

The data

The workshop uses 36 samples of Australian timber. For each sample we have its density and its hardness on the Janka scale: the force needed to push a steel ball 0.444 inches in diameter halfway into the wood. Density is easy to measure; hardness needs a testing machine. The practical question is whether density can be used to predict hardness.

The equation of a line

A straight line is described by two numbers:

\[ \hat{y} = a + bx \]

  • \(a\), the intercept, is the value of the line at x = 0.
  • \(b\), the slope, is the change in y for each one-unit increase in
  • \(\hat{y}\) (“y-hat”) is the fitted value: the value of y that the line predicts for a given x.

For example, if a = 2 and b = 0.5, then at x = 10 the line predicts \(\hat{y} = 2 + 0.5 \times 10 = 7\). The slope is measured in units of y per unit of x, so its units depend on how the variables were measured.

Residuals

Real data do not lie exactly on a line. The residual for each observation is the vertical distance between the observed value and the line:

\[ e = y - \hat{y} \]

A point above the line has a positive residual and a point below it has a negative one. Continuing the example, if the observed value at x = 10 is 8, the residual is 8 − 7 = 1.

You can draw many different straight lines through the same points, and each gives a different set of residuals. In the workshop you will draw your own line through 8 timber samples, calculate its residuals, and work out with your partner how to decide whose line is best.

What the coefficient table reports

The coefficient table has the same columns as in Session 4: estimate, standard error, t value and p-value. For the slope, t is still the estimate divided by its standard error, and the p-value tests whether the slope differs from zero. One thing changes: a straight line estimates two coefficients, the intercept and the slope, so the degrees of freedom are the number of observations minus 2.

Predicting from a line

Once you have a line, you can predict y for any value of x. The data only tell you about the range of x that was measured, though. A prediction for a value of x outside that range is called extrapolation, and it is much less trustworthy: the line has been extended into a region where there are no observations to support it.

Bring what you wrote to the session, together with a calculator, a pencil and a ruler.
