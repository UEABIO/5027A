Reading: hypotheses and the measurement

Hypotheses are claims about populations

In the last session you estimated a population quantity from a single sample and quantified your uncertainty with a confidence interval. That interval told you where the population value plausibly lies. It did not tell you whether a specific claim about the population is supported by the data. That is the job of a hypothesis test.

A statistical hypothesis is a precise claim about a population parameter. You always work with two hypotheses as a pair.

The null hypothesis (H0) states that there is no effect, no difference, or no relationship in the population. It is the claim you will try to reject. The null must be specific enough to generate predictions: if H0 is true, what data would you expect?

The alternative hypothesis (H1) states that the population parameter differs from the null value. It is what you conclude if the data are sufficiently unlikely under H0.

A hypothesis is always about the population. A statement about what happened in your sample is a description, not a hypothesis.

Is the following a valid null hypothesis?

“The mean of my sample is 5.2.”

No. A hypothesis is a claim about a population parameter, not a description of a sample quantity. The sample mean is a known number; there is nothing to test.

Directionality

The alternative hypothesis can be non-directional (two-sided) or directional (one-sided).

A two-sided alternative states that the population parameter differs from the null value, without specifying the direction. For a population mean \(\mu\):

\[H_0: \mu = 0 \qquad H_1: \mu \neq 0\]

A one-sided alternative specifies the direction of the difference:

\[H_0: \mu = 0 \qquad H_1: \mu > 0\]

The choice between one-sided and two-sided is made before looking at the data. It follows from the research question, not from the results.

A researcher asks: “Does this drug affect blood pressure?” Which alternative is appropriate?

The question asks whether the drug affects blood pressure, not whether it raises or lowers it. That is a two-sided question. If the question were “Does this drug lower blood pressure?”, the alternative would be one-sided.

The measurement: Darwin’s maize data

You already know this dataset from last time. Darwin grew fifteen pairs of maize plants, one cross-pollinated and one self-pollinated in each pair, and measured their heights. For each pair, the quantity of interest is the paired difference: the height of the cross-pollinated plant minus the height of the self-pollinated plant.

The question is whether cross-pollination increases height. Stated as hypotheses about the population mean of the paired differences, \(\mu_d\):

\[H_0: \mu_d = 0 \qquad H_1: \mu_d > 0\]

H0 claims that cross-pollination makes no difference to height in the population. H1 claims that cross-pollinated plants are taller on average.

Is this alternative one-sided or two-sided?

One-sided. Darwin’s question was specifically whether cross-pollination increases height, not merely whether it changes height. The alternative specifies a direction: \(\mu_d > 0\).

Notice that the hypotheses say nothing about the fifteen plants Darwin actually grew. They are claims about what would happen across all possible pairs of plants, of which Darwin observed fifteen.
