Null distributions and what a p-value is

This page summarises what we covered in the first hour of Session 3. Use it for revision; the full activities were worked through in class.

Progression: null hypothesis → simulate under H0 → null distribution → observed statistic → p-value.

Writing hypotheses

We began by comparing the hypotheses brought from the pre-class task. Each bench discussed one new biological scenario (not from the pre-class reading) and wrote the null and alternative hypotheses as claims about a population. Benches then stated whether their alternative was directional.

NoteThe rule

A hypothesis is a claim about a population parameter, never a description of a sample. The null hypothesis states no effect or no difference. The alternative states what you would conclude if the null were rejected. The choice of directionality follows from the research question and is fixed before looking at the data.

Check your understanding. A colleague writes: “H0: the sample means are equal.” Is this a valid null hypothesis?

No. A null hypothesis must be a claim about a population parameter, not about sample quantities. The correct form would state that the population means are equal.

The shuffle

We used Darwin’s fifteen paired differences to build a null distribution by hand. If the null hypothesis is true and cross-pollination has no effect, then each paired difference is equally likely to be positive or negative — the sign carries no information. The physical procedure was:

  1. Each bench received fifteen cards showing the observed paired differences.
  2. For each card, a coin flip decided whether to keep the original sign or reverse it.
  3. The mean of the fifteen sign-flipped differences was computed and posted on the board.
  4. Each bench repeated this three times.

The class board accumulated these means into a distribution of values that could arise if the null hypothesis were true. This distribution is the null distribution.

NoteThe rule

A null distribution is the distribution of a test statistic under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true. Each value on the board represents one outcome from a world in which cross-pollination has no effect. The observed mean difference is then compared against this distribution.

Check your understanding. In the sign-flip procedure, what justifies flipping the sign of a paired difference?

The reveal and the count

The observed mean difference was marked on the board. We then counted how many of the class-generated values were at least as far from zero as the observed value, in both directions (because a value equally extreme in the negative direction would be equally surprising under the null). That count, divided by the total number of shuffles, was the p-value.

Two points were stated explicitly at this moment:

  1. The two-tail count follows from the alternative hypothesis written before the data were seen. A one-sided alternative would count only one direction.
  2. The class proportion is an estimate of a quantity defined over all possible sign-flip assignments, not just the ones the class happened to produce.
NoteThe rule

The p-value is the proportion of values in the null distribution that are at least as extreme as the observed statistic. It is computed with respect to the alternative hypothesis stated before the data were examined.

Check your understanding. The class produced 90 shuffled means. Seven were at least as extreme as the observed difference (in either direction). The two-sided p-value is .

\(7 / 90 \approx 0.078\). The p-value is the proportion of null distribution values at least as extreme as what was observed, counting both tails.

Four statements

Each student retrieved the sentence they wrote before class (“what does statistically significant mean?”) and committed to one of four statements about what a p-value is. Discussion then identified which statement is correct and why the other three are wrong.

The four statements, in the form used in class:

Which of the following correctly states what a p-value is?

The key asymmetry: a p-value gives the probability of the data given a hypothesis, not the probability of a hypothesis given the data. This was linked back to the Bayesian stub from Session 2 — the second reading of an interval answers a different question, and computing the probability that a hypothesis is true requires a prior, which the frequentist framework does not supply.

NoteThe rule

A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as the actual data, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is not the probability that the null is true, not the probability that the result was due to chance, and not the probability that a replication would produce the same result.

Link to Session 1

We noted that both Session 1 and Session 3 built a distribution of class-generated values on the board. In Session 1, the spread came from which beans were drawn — sampling variability from a real population. In Session 3, the spread came from which signs were assigned — the variability of a null distribution constructed under H0. The two distributions answer different questions: Session 1 asked how much a statistic varies across samples; Session 3 asks how much a statistic varies when the null hypothesis is true.
