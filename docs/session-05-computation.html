Introduction

In hour 1 you drew a straight line through 8 timber samples by eye, measured how far each point missed it, and saw that least squares chooses the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals. In this workshop you will:

  • check your hand calculations in R
  • fit the same model to all 36 samples and interpret it
  • make the intercept meaningful by centring
  • check whether a straight line is good enough
  • make predictions with the right kind of interval.

The model is the same linear model you used for Darwin’s maize, fitted with lm(). The only difference is that the predictor is a continuous measurement rather than a group label:

\[ y_i = a + b x_i + e_i \]

Here \(a\) is the intercept (the value of the line at \(x = 0\)) and \(b\) is the slope (the change in \(y\) for a one-unit increase in \(x\)). The line gives the fitted value, \(\hat{y} = a + bx\), and \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) is the residual for sample \(i\): the vertical distance between the observed value and the line.

The data

The data are 36 samples of Australian timber, with wood density (dens) and hardness on the Janka scale (hardness), the force needed to embed a 0.444 inch steel ball into the wood to half its diameter.

TipWhy is the band narrowest in the middle?

A least-squares line always passes through the point (mean of x, mean of y). Near that point, the only uncertainty is about the height of the line. Further away, uncertainty about the slope matters more, because a small change in slope moves the line further the further you are from the middle. So the band widens towards both ends.

TipExplanation

The two tests give the same t because they are testing the same thing: whether there is a linear association. Choosing regression decides which variable we predict from which; it does not add evidence about cause. These samples were measured, not manipulated, so the analysis shows that denser timbers tend to be harder. A causal explanation would come from knowledge of wood structure or from an experiment.

TipWhat does this mean for the conclusions?

The positive association between density and hardness is not in doubt. The residual plot shows two problems with the straight-line description, though.

First, the curve means the line slightly underpredicts hardness at both ends of the density range and overpredicts in the middle.

Second, the residuals are more spread out at high densities than at low ones. With only 36 samples this is suggestive rather than conclusive. If the spread does increase with density, the standard errors, confidence intervals and prediction intervals are unreliable, because they all assume the same spread at every density. Prediction intervals would be too wide for light timbers and too narrow for dense ones.

Remedies, such as transforming hardness or using a model that allows the spread to change, are beyond this session. For now, the right response is to report the pattern and to be cautious about predictions at high densities.

TipExample results paragraph

Timber hardness increased with wood density (linear regression, n = 36). Each one-unit increase in density was associated with an increase of 57.5 Janka units (95% CI 52.9 to 62.1; t(34) = 25.2, p < 0.001), and density accounted for 95% of the variation in hardness (\(R^2\) = 0.95). At the mean density of 45.7, predicted hardness was 1469 (95% CI 1407 to 1531). The residuals showed slight curvature and possibly greater spread at high densities, so predictions for the densest timbers should be treated with caution.

Summary

Linear regression is the linear model you have already used, with a continuous predictor in place of a group label. Least squares chooses the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals. The slope is the change in the response for a one-unit increase in the predictor, and its t-statistic, p-value and confidence interval work exactly as they did for a difference between two means. The intercept is the value of the line at \(x = 0\); centring the predictor moves this to the mean, where it can be interpreted.

A regression of observational data measures association, not cause. Its standard errors and intervals assume a straight-line relationship with constant spread, so always check a residual plot before relying on them. Use a confidence interval to describe the mean response at a given value of the predictor, and a prediction interval to describe a single new observation. Do not predict outside the range of the data.
